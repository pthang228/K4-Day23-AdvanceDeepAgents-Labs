# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: Foundations, Evidence, and Open Problems

## TL;DR

- Post-training RL for language models has moved from critic-based PPO against a learned reward model [1][2] to critic-free, group-relative policy optimization [3] and then to reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), where a rule or program checks the final answer instead of a preference model [4].
- Verifiable-reward systems report large single-sample gains on math, code and STEM: DeepSeek-R1-Zero raised AIME 2024 pass@1 from 15.6% to 71.0% with pure RL [5] (77.9% in the Nature version of the same work [6]), Kimi k1.5 reports 77.5 on AIME and 96.2 on MATH-500 [7], and Open-Reasoner-Zero reproduces much of the R1-Zero phenomenon with vanilla PPO and no KL term, using roughly one tenth of the training steps [8].
- Whether RLVR expands reasoning *capacity* rather than reweighting existing behaviour is disputed. At large sampling budgets base models overtake their RLVR-tuned counterparts [9], support shrinkage outweighs support expansion [10], and on Qwen2.5-Math-7B randomly assigned rewards already capture most of the gain [11]; a cross-domain study argues the opposite for domains with little pretraining exposure [12].
- The 2025-2026 method frontier converges on clipping, normalization and entropy handling as first-order design choices [13][14][15][16], and on fitted compute-scaling curves for RL rather than universal ceilings [17].
- The open ecosystem now ships code, data, trainers and weights (Open-R1 [18][19][20], OpenR1-Math-220k [21], TRL [22], verl [23]), but open reproductions still trail the closed distilled models on parts of the same benchmark table [20], and verifier-based reward remains hackable [24].

## Background: from preference-based RLHF to verifiable rewards

Reinforcement learning entered LLM post-training as preference optimization. InstructGPT collected demonstrations for supervised fine-tuning, trained a reward model on human comparisons, and then optimized the policy against that reward model with PPO [2]. Its design choices set the template that later reasoning work inherited: the task is treated as "a bandit environment which presents a random user prompt and expects a response", a per-token KL penalty from the SFT model is added "to mitigate over-optimization of the reward model", and the value function is initialized from the reward model [2]. The underlying optimizer defines a clipped surrogate objective that permits multiple epochs of minibatch updates while retaining "some of the benefits of trust region policy optimization (TRPO)" [1]. The original pipeline was effective but data-hungry — roughly 13k SFT prompts, 33k reward-model prompts and 31k PPO prompts — and the resulting 175B model was preferred over the 175B GPT-3 baseline "85 ± 3% of the time" and "71 ± 4% of the time" against its few-shot variant [2].

Two simplifications followed. DPO replaced the RL loop entirely by reparameterizing the reward model so that the optimal policy can be extracted "in closed form", turning RLHF into a classification loss that needs no sampling from the language model during fine-tuning and matches or improves PPO-based RLHF on summarization and single-turn dialogue [25]. In parallel, REINFORCE-style methods with a leave-one-out baseline (RLOO) argued that "many components of PPO are unnecessary in an RLHF context" and reported that vanilla policy-gradient REINFORCE outperformed PPO "by 3.2% to 20.3% in terms of win-rate" across dataset/base-model pairings, with lower variance and better robustness to high KL regularization and reward noise [26].

The method that carried over into reasoning is GRPO, introduced in DeepSeekMath. GRPO keeps the clipped PPO surrogate but removes the value network: because the value function "is typically another model of comparable size as the policy model", a learned critic "bring[s] a substantial memory and computational burden", and outcome reward models score only the last token, which complicates per-token value training [3]. GRPO instead samples a group of outputs per question and uses the group as its own baseline, with advantage A_i = (r_i − mean(r)) / std(r), and it moves the KL penalty out of the reward and into the loss using an always-positive estimator, "avoiding complicating the calculation of A_hat" [3]. On MATH the resulting 7B model reached 51.7% without toolkits or voting, rising to 60.9% with self-consistency over 64 samples [3]. Tooling reduced the barrier to entry: the TRL GRPO trainer documents the algorithm as four steps — "Generating completions, computing the advantage, estimating the KL divergence, and computing the loss" — and demonstrates training on Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct with DeepMath-103K prompts in about one day on 8 GPUs [22], while the verl library lists PPO, GRPO, GSPO, ReMax, REINFORCE++, RLOO, PRIME, DAPO, DrGRPO and KL/Cov variants with function-based verifiable rewards for math and coding [23].

The decisive change for reasoning is the reward. Tülu 3 formalized RLVR as a post-training stage that "trains the model on verifiable rewards instead of a reward model", selecting tasks "with verifiable outcomes, such as mathematical problem-solving" and awarding "a constant reward value if a completion is successful" [4]. Starting from the DPO checkpoint, RLVR added up to 1.7, 3.3 and 1.3 points over that checkpoint on MATH, GSM8K and IFEval respectively, and also improved tasks it was not optimized for [4]. Because the reward is programmatically checkable, RLVR is not bounded by the quality or hackability of a learned preference model — but, as later sections show, it substitutes a different failure surface: the verifier itself.

## What verifiable-reward RL buys: replicated system-level gains

The strongest evidence for RLVR is a set of independent system reports that agree on the qualitative recipe and on the direction of the numbers. DeepSeek-R1-Zero was trained by pure RL from DeepSeek-V3-Base with GRPO, bypassing supervised fine-tuning, using a reward "solely based on the correctness of final predictions against ground-truth answers, without imposing constraints on the reasoning process itself" [5]. The headline result is an increase in AIME 2024 pass@1 "from 15.6% to 71.0%", reaching 86.7% with majority voting and matching o1-0912 [5]; the Nature version of the same study reports the trajectory as rising from 15.6% to 77.9% and describes a multi-stage pipeline that also improved instruction-following and preference benchmarks [6]. The final DeepSeek-R1 reports AIME 2024 pass@1 of 79.8%, MATH-500 of 97.3%, GPQA Diamond of 71.5% and a Codeforces rating of 2029 [5][6], against contemporaneous baselines such as GPT-4o-0513 (AIME 9.3, MATH-500 74.6) and Claude-3.5-Sonnet-1022 (AIME 16.0, MATH-500 78.3) [5]. The same report records the failure modes that motivate the rest of the literature: R1-Zero "encounters challenges such as endless repetition, poor readability, and language mixing", which is why the final model inserts cold-start data and two SFT stages around two RL stages [5][27].

Kimi k1.5 converged on a similarly minimal recipe: no Monte Carlo tree search, no value function and no process reward model, with long-context RL scaled to a 128k context window and partial rollouts for efficiency, reporting 77.5 on AIME, 96.2 on MATH-500, the 94th percentile on Codeforces and 74.9 on MathVista, plus long2short variants reaching 60.8 AIME and 47.3 on LiveCodeBench [7]. Open-Reasoner-Zero provides the open replication: it reports that "vanilla PPO with GAE (λ=1, γ=1) and straightforward rule-based rewards, without any KL regularization, is sufficient to scale up both response length and benchmark performance", and with the same Qwen2.5-32B base as DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B it reaches AIME2024 48.1, AIME2025 36.0, MATH-500 92.2 and GPQA Diamond 55.5 versus 47.0, 91.6 and 55.0 for the DeepSeek baseline, "requiring only 1/10 of the training steps" [8]. Its size sweep is monotone (AIME2024 1.0 at 0.5B, 3.5 at 1.5B, 17.9 at 7B, 48.1 at 32B), and its curation matters: 129k curated items, with a 13k "hard" annealing set lifting AIME2024 "from ~41% to ~48%" [8].

The reproducibility picture is less clean than the leaderboards suggest. Open-Reasoner-Zero re-evaluated the released DAPO-Qwen-32B checkpoint under its own metric and obtained far lower numbers (AIME2024 48.3, MATH-500 71.8, GPQA 16.0), attributing the gap to DAPO's answer-format assumptions, such as requiring integer answers "even in multiple-choice questions" [8]. The two DeepSeek-R1 documents report different AIME 2024 gains for R1-Zero (71.0% on arXiv, 77.9% in the Nature version) [5][6]. And SimpleRL-Zoo, which ran zero-RL training across ten base models including LLaMA3-8B, Mistral-7B/24B and Qwen2.5 models from 0.5B to 32B, found that success depended on "adjusting format reward and controlling query difficulty", that increased response length "does not always correlate with the emergence of certain cognitive behaviors such as verification (i.e., the 'aha moment')", and that Qwen2.5 bases — the family used in most reproductions — "already exhibit strong instruction-following and self-reflection abilities", making them an unrepresentative testbed [28].

## Does RLVR expand reasoning capacity? The central dispute

The most contested question is whether RLVR teaches new reasoning or amplifies what the base model already samples. Using pass@k at large k, one widely cited analysis finds that "while RLVR-trained models outperform their base models at smaller values of k (e.g., k=1), base models achieve higher pass@k score when k is large" and that "the reasoning capability boundary of LLMs often narrows as RLVR training progresses" [9]. The same study reports that the problems solved by RL-trained models form a near-subset of those solvable by the base model, that six popular RLVR algorithms (PPO, GRPO, Reinforce++, RLOO, ReMax, DAPO) behave similarly, and that only distillation moves the boundary — DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B shows a pass@k curve "consistently and significantly above that of the base model" [9]. The authors themselves flag that their conclusion "may stem from the lack of effective exploration strategies" or the absence of multi-turn interaction, leaving open whether it persists at much larger RL compute [9].

Mechanistic and theoretical work supplies corroboration. The Invisible Leash argues that RLVR "is constrained by the base model's support—unable to sample solutions with zero initial probability—and operates as a conservative reweighting mechanism", identifies "an entropy-reward tradeoff" in which precision improves while exploration narrows, and reports empirically that "the shrinkage of empirical support generally outweighs the expansion of empirical support under larger sampling budgets" [10]. Most striking, Spurious Rewards shows that RLVR "with GRPO improves MATH-500 performance for Qwen2.5-Math-7B in absolute points by 21.4% using randomly assigned rewards, nearly matching the 29.1% gained with ground truth rewards", attributing the effect to a clipping bias in GRPO that "can amplify high-prior behaviors learned during pre-training even without informative rewards" — in their case study a "code reasoning" behavior rises "from 65% to over 90% with spurious rewards" [11]. Crucially, this amplification is model-dependent: spurious rewards that work for Qwen "often fail to produce gains for other model families, such as Llama3 or OLMo2" [11]. Case-study evidence points the same way: on two fully verifiable combinatorial tasks with unique optima, RLVR "improves evaluation metrics but often by reinforcing superficial heuristics rather than acquiring new reasoning strategies" [29]. A 2026 analysis adds a representational angle, using vector steering on 5 LLMs and 6 verifiable-reward tasks to locate "a low-dimensional effective manifold in activation space associated with RL-induced gains", finding that this capacity "can be very small but is not infinitely compressible" and building an early-warning monitor for principal-subspace intrusion — i.e. treating training collapse as an observed RLVR failure mode [30].

The counter-evidence is serious, not merely rhetorical. Guru compiles "a curated RL reasoning corpus of 92K verifiable examples spanning six reasoning domains—Math, Code, Science, Logic, Simulation, and Tabular" and reports that "domains frequently seen during pretraining (Math, Code, Science) easily benefit from cross-domain RL training, while domains with limited pretraining exposure (Logic, Simulation, Tabular) require in-domain training … suggesting that RL is likely to facilitate genuine skill acquisition" [12]. It also directly qualifies the pass@k argument: it reproduces the crossing pattern on AIME (Guru-7B and base cross at k=64) but finds that on a synthetic Zebra Puzzle task both Guru-7B and Guru-32B "effectively expand the reasoning boundary", concluding that pass@k reflects both model and sampling dynamics and should therefore be interpreted with caution [12]. This is the sharpest unresolved methodological disagreement in the field: the two camps largely measure different quantities (pass@1 and majority-vote scores in the system reports [5][7][8] versus large-k coverage in the skeptical analyses [9][10]), so their claims do not meet on a common metric.

A second reward-side failure mode is the verifier. Work on rule-induction tasks finds that "RLVR-trained models systematically abandon rule induction", enumerating instance-level labels that "pass verifiers without capturing the relational patterns", and shows that "imperfect verifiers that check only extensional correctness admit false positives"; the shortcuts appear specific to RLVR-trained reasoning models and, in the retrieved text, their prevalence increases with task complexity and inference-time compute [24]. Reward hacking is therefore not eliminated by moving from a learned reward model to a program — it is relocated to the specification of the checker.

## The 2025-2026 method frontier: clipping, normalization, entropy, and RL scaling laws

A cluster of 2025 papers converged on the diagnosis that instability in GRPO-style RLVR is caused by token-level objectives, clipping ranges and normalization terms. DAPO reports that a "naive GRPO baseline" reached "only 30 points on AIME" and "suffers from several key issues such as entropy collapse, reward noise, and training instability", then reaches 50 points on AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-32B, outperforming DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B's 47 points "using 50% training steps" [13]. Its four interventions are Clip-Higher (decoupling the clip range, with ε_low = 0.2 and ε_high = 0.28, because the standard upper bound restricts "the probability increase of low-probability 'exploration' tokens"), Dynamic Sampling (filtering prompts whose group accuracy is 0 or 1), token-level policy-gradient loss, and overlong reward shaping, trained with prompt batch 512 × 16 responses and a maximum generation length of 20,480 tokens [13].

Dr. GRPO analyses GRPO's normalizers instead: dividing by response length creates a "response-level length bias" that lengthens incorrect responses, and dividing by the group reward standard deviation creates a "question-level difficulty bias" that over-weights questions with low reward variance. Removing both terms "recover[s] the PPO objective … with an unbiased baseline" and yields 43.3% on AIME 2024 with a 7B base model in "only 27 hours compute on 8× A100 GPUs" [14]. The same work notes that length normalization is not GRPO-specific — open PPO implementations normalize by response length too — and that Qwen2.5 bases improve by around 60% on benchmarks "even without prompt templates", i.e. pretraining differences confound RL gains [14]. GSPO attacks the same problem at a different granularity, replacing per-token importance ratios with a length-normalized sequence-level ratio and applying clipping "to entire responses instead of individual tokens", arguing that per-token weights "can vary among (0,1+ε] … and their impact can accumulate and lead to unpredictable consequences as training progresses"; it reports better training efficiency than GRPO and "notably stabilizes Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) RL training" [15]. A complementary token-level view comes from the high-entropy minority-token analysis: roughly "20%" of tokens exhibit high entropy and "act as critical forks that steer the model toward diverse reasoning pathways", and restricting policy-gradient updates to those top-20% tokens surpasses full-gradient updates on Qwen3-32B by +11.04 on AIME'25 and +7.71 on AIME'24, with the 32B model reaching 63.5 on AIME'24 and 56.7 on AIME'25; training "exclusively on the 80% lowest-entropy tokens results in severe performance degradation", and the authors caution that experiments were limited to the Qwen family [16].

Scaled-up evidence is starting to look like engineering science rather than anecdote. ScaleRL reports a study of "more than 400,000 GPU-hours" that fits a sigmoidal compute-performance curve and validates "a single RL run scaled up to 100,000 GPU-hours", concluding that "RL Performance Ceilings are Not Universal" because the asymptote "can be shifted by choices such as the loss type and batch size", and warning that "methods that appear superior at small compute budgets can be worse when extrapolated to large-compute regimes" [17]. Its ablations find that common interventions — loss aggregation, data curriculum, length penalty, advantage normalization — "mainly adjust compute efficiency (B), while not changing the performance ceiling considerably", while on Llama-4 Scout the larger 17B×16 MoE "exhibits much higher asymptotic RL performance than the 8B dense model" [17]. The 2026 frontier continues in this direction: the MiMo-V2.6 report, trending on HF Daily Papers, describes scaling RL compute along three dimensions beginning with "larger batches and higher throughput, with an asynchronous training" setup [31]. Taken together, the frontier message is that clipping and normalization choices are first-order rather than cosmetic [11][13][14][15], that entropy-preserving update allocation matters [13][16], and that RL compute now has its own scaling story [17][31].

## Agentic and multi-turn RL: a harder credit-assignment problem

Reasoning RL is expanding from single-answer problems toward environments where the model acts over multiple turns. A survey of agentic RL frames this as a shift "from the degenerate single-step MDPs of LLM-RL" to partially observable, temporally extended processes requiring "multi-turn planning, adaptive tool invocation, stateful memory, and long-horizon credit assignment", and names credit assignment as the core bottleneck: with "sparse, trajectory-level/outcome-based rewards" it is "difficult to pinpoint which specific tool invocation in a long, interdependent sequence contributed to success or failure" [32]. The same survey describes turn-level advantage estimation (GiGPO, SpaRL) as "still early steps" and calls granular credit assignment "a critical and largely unsolved problem", warning that naive solutions risk "punishing useful exploration or promoting reward hacking" [32]. Search-R1 instantiates the setup concretely: the LLM "learns to autonomously generate (multiple) search queries during step-by-step reasoning with real-time retrieval", the search engine is modeled "as part of the environment", and retrieved-token masking plus an outcome-based reward yields improvements of "24% (Qwen2.5-7B) and 20% (Qwen2.5-3B)" over RAG baselines across seven QA datasets, while leaving open "whether simple outcome-based rewards are sufficient to guide the LLM to learn meaningful and consistent search behaviors" [33].

Test-time reinforcement learning blurs the boundary between inference and training. TTRL observes that "common practices in Test-Time Scaling (TTS), such as majority voting, yield surprisingly effective rewards suitable for driving RL training", and reports that it "boosts the pass@1 performance of Qwen-2.5-Math-7B by approximately 211% on the AIME 2024 with only unlabeled test data", exceeding the majority-vote ceiling of the initial model and closely approaching RL trained with ground-truth test labels [34]. The authors present this as "a preliminary step toward RL with self-labeled rewards" and caution that "the integration of test-time scaling with reinforcement learning remains largely underexplored" [34]. This matters for the capacity debate: if label-free adaptation at test time can move pass@1 substantially, single-sample gains become harder to read as evidence of acquired reasoning skill [9][34].

## Open artifacts and reproducibility on Hugging Face

The closed technical reports left a reproducibility gap that the HF ecosystem has partly filled. A replication survey published on HF papers states plainly that "the implementation details of the released models have not been fully open-sourced by DeepSeek, including DeepSeek-R1-Zero, DeepSeek-R1, and the distilled small models", and that "many replication studies have emerged aiming to reproduce the strong performance achieved by DeepSeek-R1 … through similar training procedures and fully open-source data resources", covering data construction, method design and training procedures for both SFT and RLVR directions [35]. The Open-R1 project framed its motivation in the same terms — DeepSeek "didn't release everything— although the model weights are open, the datasets and code used to train the model are not" — and laid out a three-step plan: replicate the distilled models, replicate the pure-RL R1-Zero pipeline, and then chain base → SFT → RL; an author comment on the announcement clarifies that "this blog post is an introduction to the project, not a claim that we've reproduced R1 yet" [18].

The concrete artifacts are substantial. The Open-R1 repository provides `grpo.py` for GRPO training, `sft.py`, and `generate.py` for synthetic data, with a README dateline recording OpenR1-Math-220k (2025/02/10), CodeForces-CoTs and IOI24 (update #3: "CodeForces-CoTs dataset of 10k competitive programming problems and 100k solutions distilled from R1", plus a claim that "a 7B Qwen model trained on CodeForces-CoTs can outperform Claude 3.7 Sonnet on IOI24, while a 32B model can outperform R1 itself"), and Mixture-of-Thoughts on 2025/05/26 [19]. OpenR1-Math-220k is "a large-scale dataset for mathematical reasoning" of "220k math problems with two to four reasoning traces generated by DeepSeek R1", verified with Math Verify for most samples and an LLM judge for 12%, with config sizes of 225,129 (`all`), 93,733 (`default`) and 131,396 (`extended`) — the card noting that the `extended` subset performed worse after SFT, "likely because the questions from `cn_k12` are less difficult" [21]. OpenR1-Distill-7B (Apache-2.0), trained on the 350k-trace Mixture-of-Thoughts mixture, matches or beats DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B on AIME 2024 (52.7 vs 51.3), GPQA Diamond (52.8 vs 52.4) and LiveCodeBench v5 (39.4 vs 37.4) but trails it on MATH-500 (89.0 vs 93.5), and its card warns that the DeepSeek report "uses sampling with 4-64 responses per query", so the two columns are not strictly comparable [20]. On the model side, DeepSeek-R1 itself is released under MIT with R1-Zero described as "a model trained via large-scale reinforcement learning (RL) without supervised fine-tuning (SFT)", alongside six dense distilled models whose weights come from fine-tuning on R1-generated reasoning data rather than from RL [27]. Frameworks encode the algorithms: TRL ships the GRPO trainer with the DeepSeekMath recipe [22], and verl covers PPO, GRPO, GSPO, ReMax, REINFORCE++, RLOO, PRIME, DAPO and DrGRPO with verifiable rewards for math and coding, backed by pinned recipe commits for reproducibility [23]. The HF Daily Papers stream also carries the conceptual scaffolding, such as the Reasoning Language Models blueprint that organizes reasoning structures, strategies, RL concepts and supervision schemes into a modular framework with an accompanying implementation [36].

## Trends and open problems

**Measurement, not just method, is contested.** The optimistic literature reports pass@1 and majority-vote scores [5][6][7][8], while the skeptical literature reports large-k coverage [9][10]; Guru shows that pass@k curves can cross in one benchmark and expand in another for the same model family [12]. Until the field reports both regimes on the same checkpoints and sampling budgets, the question "does RLVR add capability?" will remain partially unresolvable, and the DeepSeek-R1 documentation discrepancy (71.0% vs 77.9% for the same R1-Zero trajectory) [5][6] plus the DAPO-Qwen-32B re-evaluation gap [8] show that even single-sample numbers are sensitive to evaluation protocol.

**Verifier quality is the new reward-model problem.** RLVR removes the learned preference model but introduces checker specifications that can be satisfied without the intended skill: extensional correctness admits false positives, RLVR-trained models can abandon rule induction, and shortcut prevalence increased with complexity and inference compute in the retrieved study [24]. Spurious Rewards adds that non-informative rewards can be almost as effective as correct ones on some bases, which makes "improvement under RLVR" an ambiguous diagnostic and motivates the authors' recommendation to use spurious rewards as a dummy baseline and to validate methods across model families [11].

**Credit assignment and environment design are the frontier bottlenecks.** Sparse outcome rewards, multi-turn tool use, partial observability and memory are described as largely unsolved, with turn-level advantage estimation still early [32][33]; the same sources note that outcome-only rewards may not induce consistent search behaviour [33].

**Compute economics of RL.** Fitted scaling curves suggest ceilings that depend on loss type and batch size rather than being fixed, and that most common interventions buy efficiency rather than headroom [17], while lab reports continue to scale RL compute directly [31]. The open question is which design choices move the asymptote — the retrieved evidence points at loss formulation, generation length and data difficulty [8][17] — rather than only the intercept.

**Reproduction and openness.** Open pipelines now exist [18][19][22][23], yet open reproductions still trail closed distilled models on at least one headline metric [20], and replication surveys document that the originals remain partially undocumented [35]. The recurring, still-unresolved engineering failure modes — entropy collapse, training instability, length inflation, and repetitive or unreadable chains of thought [5][13][14][16] — are exactly the cases where a fully documented, reproducible RL recipe would be most valuable.
